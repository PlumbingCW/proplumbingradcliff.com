How to Install a Garbage Disposal Sink Flange in Radcliff, KY Without Leaks

By the Radcliff Pro Plumbing teamUpdated October 29, 2026

The flange is the part that seals the disposal to the sink. Get the seal and the tightening order right and the rest of the install is mostly lifting and lining up.

Call (855) 575-2890

What the sink flange does

The sink flange is the metal collar that drops into the drain opening of a kitchen sink and holds the disposal underneath. A bead of sealant under its lip keeps water out of the cabinet. When a new disposal drips from the top of the unit or around the drain basket, the flange seal is the first place to look.

A first install usually takes a couple of hours with the right tools, and the flange step is the one that decides whether the cabinet stays dry. Most disposals ship with the flange, a fiber gasket, a backup ring, a mounting ring and a snap ring already in the box.

Tools and parts to lay out first

Gather everything before you start so the sealant does not skin over while you hunt for a screwdriver.

  • Plumber's putty or silicone, as your sink and disposal instructions allow
  • Flat screwdriver and a hammer or mallet for the mounting lugs
  • Channel-lock pliers and a bucket for the old trap and drain
  • Hacksaw if the new discharge tube needs trimming
  • Dishwasher drain hose clamp, if a dishwasher connects to the unit
  • A flashlight, gloves and a few rags

Putty or silicone under the flange

Both seal well when applied correctly. The difference is the sink material and how long you can wait before using it.

FactorPlumber's puttySilicone sealant
Best forStainless steel and most cast iron sinksNatural stone, some composite and sinks where putty is warned against
Cure timeNone, but wait to run water per the labelNeeds hours to set before water
CleanupTrims and wipes with a ragMessier and harder to remove
Stain riskOils can stain porous stoneLow on most surfaces
Later removalEasy to scrape and redoTakes cutting and solvent

Read the sink maker's note first. Some stone and solid-surface sinks specify silicone, and the disposal manufacturer may name a preferred sealant too.

Step by step: seat the flange and hang the disposal

Unplug the old disposal, or switch off its circuit at the breaker panel if it is wired directly, before any work starts.

1

Clear the old unit

Support the disposal, release the mounting ring by turning its lugs, lower it out, and remove the old flange and ring from above and below.

2

Clean and seal

Wipe the drain opening dry, roll a rope of putty about as thick as a pencil, and press it around the underside of the new flange lip.

3

Seat the flange

Drop it into the opening, press down, then add the gasket, backup ring and mounting ring from underneath.

4

Fit the snap ring

Seat the snap ring in its groove on the flange sleeve, then tighten the three mounting screws evenly until the flange sits flat and putty squeezes out.

5

Wipe and hang

Trim the squeezed-out putty from above, lift the disposal onto the mounting ring, and turn the lugs until it locks.

Reconnect the discharge and the dishwasher

Line up the disposal's discharge elbow with the trap, with its rubber gasket seated. Tighten the elbow bolts by hand and finish with a quarter turn of the pliers, since overtightening can split the gasket.

If a dishwasher drains through the disposal, knock out the plug inside the dishwasher inlet with a screwdriver and hammer before you attach the hose. Skipping this step is the most common reason a new disposal install leaves a dishwasher full of water. Fish the knocked-out plug out of the grinding chamber afterward.

Check that the new disposal sits level in relation to the trap. A strained joint will leak later even if it looks dry today.

Run the leak test

Fill the sink basin about halfway, then open the stopper and watch the flange, the mounting ring and every joint below with a dry paper towel. Repeat with the disposal running and cold water flowing. Check again after an hour and the next morning. A damp towel at any joint tells you which one to snug or re-seal.

Where a new install usually leaks

Where the drip appears points at the cause.

Drip locationLikely causeFix
Top of the unit, around the drain basketPutty too thin or flange not tightened evenlyRemove, re-seal, and tighten in rotation
Seam between flange and disposal bodyGasket or snap ring not seatedReseat gasket and ring
Discharge elbowMissing or pinched rubber gasketReplace gasket and retighten
Dishwasher hose connectionLoose clampTighten or replace clamp
Trap joints belowMisaligned or cross-threaded slip nutsRe-fit with new washers
Body of the disposal itselfCracked housing or failed internal sealReplace the disposal

Check the drain before you blame the disposal

A disposal that drains slowly or backs up into the second sink bowl may have nothing wrong with the flange. A greasy trap or a partial clog in the line past it will do the same thing. If water pools in the sink after a clean install, have the line looked at with a professional drain cleaning visit before assuming the new unit is faulty.

A cabinet that smells musty after the install should be dried and rechecked. Slow seepage soaks particleboard and shows up weeks later as swelling at the base.

When to hire a licensed plumber

The flange swap is a reasonable project. These situations are not:

  • The sink is cracked, chipped around the opening, or the drain hole is not standard size.
  • The trap and drain pipes do not line up and need cutting, new fittings or a re-pitch.
  • There is no outlet under the sink and the unit needs new wiring, which is electrician work.
  • Pipes are old, corroded or bound together with rusted nuts that will not budge.
  • You are adding a disposal to a sink that never had one and the drain needs rework.
